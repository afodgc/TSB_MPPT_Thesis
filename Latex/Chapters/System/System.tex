\fancychapter{System}
\label{chap:System}

This chapter presents the electrical architecture of \ac{sg01}, providing the context needed for developing and integrating the proposed \ac{mppt} charger. It presents the main subsystems relevant to solar energy conversion, such as the installed solar panels and battery. The impact of the \ac{pv} system is analyzed, as well as the environmental changes. 

\section{Overview}

\ac{sg01} is the most recent vessel built by \acs{tsb}, designed to compete in the Sea Lab category of the Monaco Energy Boat Challenge. It uses hydrofoils to hydroplane and reduce drag. As it is \acs{tsb}'s largest and heaviest vessel to date, it required more power, addressed with a 130~kW motor combined with the first ever \acs{tsb} High Voltage system (300-600~V), paired with a Low Voltage system (30-50~V) for the auxiliary electronics. Since solar panels alone could never meet the motor's power needs, the High Voltage system is designed to be powered by hydrogen instead, however, this is not yet implemented due to budget constraints, while the Low Voltage system relies on solar energy. Figure~\ref{fig:SG01_sch} shows a highly simplified diagram of the overall system architecture just described.

\begin{figure} [!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.80 \textwidth]{Images/System/Sys_diagram.pdf}
    \qquad
    \caption{Simplified electrical schematic of \ac{sg01}.}
    \label{fig:SG01_sch}
\end{figure}

The low voltage system is designed to power all auxiliary system (dashboard, telemetry, flight control, electrical string, throttle, etc.). Some of these auxiliary systems are critical for the normal use of the vessel; for example, if the dashboard turns off, the pilot will not know the state of the high voltage system (temperature, voltage, state of charge, etc.). Or, even more critically, if the throttle does not work, the boat cannot move electrically.

Auxiliary systems are always changing and growing, so it is difficult to estimate their power consumption. For this work it is considered 1000~W of continuous consumption. It is not a perfect estimation because the power consumption will depend on the driving mode (autonomous driving, hydroplaning, or normal drive), which can create different power profiles due to the actuators. Additionally, the cooling system (which is planned to be moved to the High Voltage system) has high power consumption that is not considered in this estimation. 

To help keeping the low voltage system always powered, solar panels are used and connected to several \acp{mppt}. In the future, there are plans to add a bidirectional DC-DC converter between the low voltage battery and the high voltage battery, enabling energy transfer in both directions. 


\section{PV system}

The photovoltaic system of \ac{sg01} was designed to maximize the energy harvested from the available deck area while maintaining reliable operation under the varying environmental conditions encountered in marine applications.

The current installation consists of 140 Maxeon Gen 3 (Me3) photovoltaic cells distributed among ten custom manufactured solar panels. These panels connect to four independent \acp{mppt}, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:PV_SG01}. The distribution of panels among the \acp{mppt} was carefully selected to reduce the effects of partial shading, temperature gradients, and non uniform solar irradiance across the vessel surface. By electrically separating regions with different operating conditions, each \ac{mppt} can independently track the optimal operating point of its associated panels, increasing the overall energy yield.

Each \ac{pv} cell has an active area of approximately $155.06~\mathrm{cm^2}$, resulting in a total photovoltaic area of $2.171~\mathrm{m^2}$.
Assuming \ac{stc} (irradiance of $1000~\mathrm{W/m^2}$ and $ 25^\circ\mathrm {C} $), the photovoltaic array is capable of delivering approximately $526.4~\mathrm{W}$ of peak power (theoretical value). 

The \ac{pv} energy is extracted by four commercial Genasun \acp{mppt}, grouped in a dedicated \ac{mppt} box, which convert the variable solar panel voltage into a stable charging voltage. This architecture allows each photovoltaic group to independently track its \ac{mpp}, improving energy extraction under non uniform illumination conditions.

To ensure high performance and shape configurability, the photovoltaic modules were manufactured in house using materials selected specifically for marine operation. This process begins by soldering all cells in a module together, then laminating them using vacuum and heat blankets.

Prior to installation, all panels underwent electroluminescence testing to detect manufacturing defects such as microcracks, broken cells, or interconnection failures, Figure~\ref{fig:ilu_test}. These tests were performed before integration into the vessel to ensure that only panels with acceptable electrical and structural integrity were installed. This quality control procedure reduces the likelihood of premature degradation and enables comparison before and after competitions.

Figure~\ref{fig:renderSG01} presents a conceptual rendering with supplementary solar panels. Preliminary estimates indicate that the total photovoltaic power could be increased to approximately $977~\mathrm{W}$, almost doubling the currently installed capacity. This upgrade would substantially reduce battery charging times and significantly increase the vessel's autonomy.


\section{Battery}
\label{sec:battery}
In recent years, we have been producing our batteries, achieving a lower cost and a highly customizable solution. In \ac{sg01} we chose lithium-ion cells due to their ease of use and quick assembly. The battery assembly is done by the team members, and it begins by placing the cells on a plastic holder to increase structural strength, then using a spot welder, the series and parallel connections are made with nickel tabs (Figure~\ref{fig:SG01_battery}). In parallel, some thermistors are placed in different locations of the battery to ensure a precise and robust temperature reading. In the end, the battery is placed in a case with a \ac{bms} and all necessary connections.

\section{PV System Impact on Autonomy}

With \ac{sg01} battery (5844.56~Wh) and the maximum power that the solar panels can currently provide (526.4~W), the minimum time to fully charge the battery is 11 h and 7 min (5844.56~Wh / 526.4~W). This is a long time to charge a battery. There are two solutions to this problem: increase the power provided by the solar panels or decrease the battery capacity.

Let's consider reducing the battery capacity to 1512~Wh, the capacity of our solar boat (\acs{sr03}) battery. With this change alone, the battery is fully charged in 2 h and 53 min, which is a much more reasonable time. Additionally, if we increase the power provided by the solar panels to 977 W, the battery will be fully charged in 1 h and 33 min, which is an excellent time to charge a battery.


The charging times are good for evaluating the \ac{pv} system, but the main goal is to maximize the time on water before full battery discharge. For that, let's consider 1000~W of consumption. Considering the original battery, it takes 5~h 51~min to fully discharge without solar panels, 12~h 21min with solar panels, and approximately 10 days with the additional solar panels. As previously said, this battery is overdimensioned and will be changed in the future.

To better understand the impact of the \ac{pv} system, let's consider the battery of \acs{sr03}. This battery takes 1~h and 30~min to fully discharge without solar panels, 3~h 12~min with solar panels, and approximately 2 days 18~h with the additional solar panels. 

By reducing the battery capacity and increasing the solar panel area, the battery would never need external charging. From another point of view, the low voltage system can drain more power from the battery.



\section{Environmental changes}

In engineering, the term ``fast'', used in the title of this thesis, needs to be associated with a time scale. A process can only be considered fast when compared with another process with a longer characteristic time. Therefore, this section investigates the characteristic time scales associated with \ac{mpp} variations caused by environmental changes, with particular emphasis on the vessel's oscillatory motion and its implications for the required speed of the \ac{mppt} algorithm.

When analyzing \ac{mpp} variations, two main factors are temperature and irradiance. Temperature variations are generally slower than variations in irradiance. Although rapid temperature changes can occur, for example when water comes into contact with the photovoltaic panels, such events are considered unusual during normal operation and are therefore not considered in this analysis. Irradiation, by contrast, can vary more rapidly as the angle of the solar panels changes with vessel roll and pitch. 

The motion of a vessel subjected to waves can be described as a forced dynamic system. The vessel has characteristic natural frequencies associated with its different modes of motion, including roll, pitch and heave. The response to wave excitation depends on the relationship between the excitation frequency and the vessel's corresponding natural frequency. In particular, when the excitation frequency approaches a natural frequency, the response amplitude can increase significantly due to resonance. The vessel does not, however, necessarily oscillate at its natural frequency when subjected to excitation at another frequency, the steady state response occurs primarily at the excitation frequency, while the natural frequency determines the dynamic response and its amplitude.

Considering a simplified undamped free oscillation system, the natural frequency of a vessel can be calculated using the natural period Formula~\ref{eq:fundamental period} where $I$ is the mass moment of inertia and $C$ is the restoring stiffness \cite{rao2011mechanical}.

\begin{equation}
    T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{I}{C}}
    \label{eq:fundamental period}
\end{equation}

When applied to pitch and roll, the restoring stiffness is given by the mass (m), gravity (g) and metacentric height ($GM_L$ for pitch and $GM_t$ for roll). Since the inertia mass was not given, the equation was further simplified using the radius of gyration in pitch or roll ($k_{yy}$ or $k_{xx}$), which is equivalent to Formula~\ref{eq:radius of gyration}.

\begin{equation}
    k = \sqrt{\frac{I}{m}}
    \label{eq:radius of gyration}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{I}{mgGM}} \leftrightarrow T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{k_{yy}^2}{gGM_L}}
    \label{eq: natural period}
\end{equation}

For pitch, $k=k_{yy}$ and $GM=GM_L$, whereas for roll, $k=k_{xx}$ and $GM=GM_T$.

This formula is used with results given by the hydrostatic simulations, which were provided by \ac{tsb}, where $GM_L$ is $21.515~\mathrm{m}$ and $GM_t$ is $1.867~\mathrm{m}$. The radius of gyration is also calculated based on the longitude (L=8~m) and width/beam (B=2.5~m). Based on the International Towing Tank Conference (ITTC) recommendation \cite{ittc_seakeeping_2024}, when the radius of gyration for pitch and roll is unknown, use 0.25L and 0.35B to 0.40B, respectively.

Based on the given values, the pitch and roll natural periods are 0.8654 and 1.2853, respectively, which is equivalent to 1.1555~Hz and 0.7780~Hz. These values represent an estimate of the natural frequencies of the vessel.

As for the frequency of the waves, it has a wide range and depends on several factors. Studies on this subject show higher energy for waves with 1 to 30 seconds periods, which is equivalent to 0.033~Hz to 1~Hz Figure~\ref{fig:wave by period}.

The environmental excitation experienced by \ac{sg01} was further investigated using experimental data collected while the vessel was adrift at sea. Ten datasets containing the roll and pitch angles were collected and combined to obtain the frequency domain response shown in Figure~\ref{fig:sg01 wave}. The results show significant spectral components at approximately 0.05~Hz and 0.5~Hz. The lower frequency likely reflects fast winds or transient oscillations and is consistent with the local forecast wave period. The higher frequency can be caused by the natural frequency of the vessel. The root causes were not investigated further, as this falls outside the scope of this project.

Both theoretical and experimental results predict or measure frequency up to 1~Hz. In the context of the \ac{mppt} algorithm, the goal will be a minimum speed of 1~Hz or 1 second and a typical speed of more than 2~Hz or 0.5 seconds.

These values will directly influence the sampling time and algorithm working frequency, as it will discuss later.
